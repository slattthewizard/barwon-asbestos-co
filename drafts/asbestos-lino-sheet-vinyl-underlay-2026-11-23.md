# Asbestos Lino, Sheet Vinyl and Underlay: Test Before You Rip It Up

Old floors get ripped up all the time. A tenant moves out, a buyer wants floorboards, someone's started a bathroom reno and the lino is lifting at the edges. It looks like the easiest job in the house: grab a corner, pull, scrape what's left, bin it.

With flooring from the right era, that's the moment you want to stop and think. The backing layer of some old sheet floor coverings is commonly reported as asbestos-containing, and some old underlay and adhesives are listed as places asbestos may be found. You can't tell by looking at it, and the way you take it up changes how risky it is. This guide covers sheet lino and vinyl, backing, underlay and adhesive. If you're after vinyl floor tiles, we've covered those separately in our guide to [where asbestos hides in an old Geelong house](/blog/where-asbestos-hides-old-geelong-house/).

## Why old sheet flooring is on the list

Asbestos was used in a huge range of Australian building products. Asbestos cement came into common use in housing from the mid-1940s and stayed until the late 1980s, and a total ban on all forms of asbestos came into force on 31 December 2003. A house built or extended before 1990 may contain asbestos, and that includes materials that aren't sheeting at all.

Sheet floor coverings are one of those materials. Lino and old sheet vinyl are made in layers: a wear surface on top and a backing underneath. The backing layer of some old sheet floor coverings was commonly reported as containing asbestos. That's what the older guidance says, and it's reason enough to be careful. It doesn't mean every old floor has it, and it doesn't tell you which ones do. Only a lab test can confirm.

Because the asbestos, where it's present, is in the backing and not the pretty top layer, the surface can look perfectly fine. A glossy pattern in good nick tells you nothing about what's under it.

## Why the backing matters most

Intact, glued down and left alone, old sheet flooring isn't doing anything. The risk comes when it's disturbed.

Asbestos cement is usually non-friable, meaning the fibres are bound in the cement. A backing layer can behave differently. Once a floor covering gets torn, cracked, curled, scraped or peeled in strips, the backing can break down into a fibrous, crumbly or papery layer, and that can be the friable kind. Friable asbestos can be crushed by hand and releases fibres into the air much more easily, and in Victoria it's Class A work that always needs a licensed removalist, with no exemption at any quantity. Our guide to [friable versus non-friable asbestos](/blog/friable-vs-non-friable-asbestos/) explains the distinction.

So the usual DIY approach of tearing it up in strips and scraping the remnants off the floor is exactly the technique that creates the problem.

## Carpet underlay and adhesives

Old carpet underlay is sometimes listed as a place asbestos may be found. If you've lifted a corner of old carpet and found a layer of old felt-like or paper-like underlay beneath it, particularly on a house built before 1990, don't assume it's harmless just because it isn't a sheet of fibro. People searching for "asbestos carpet underlay" or "asbestos underlay" are usually in the middle of a renovation, and it's a fair question.

Adhesives are the other piece. The glue or bitumen-style adhesive that holds old sheet flooring to a slab or to boards can also be listed as a possible asbestos-containing material, and it's the stuff left behind after the covering comes up. Scraping or sanding it back to a clean surface is a common DIY step. That is another job to hold off on until testing is done.

The short version is that these are materials where asbestos may be present, not materials that always contain it. The sensible position is: unknown until tested.

## What not to do

- **Don't rip it up dry.** Pulling up old sheet flooring in strips breaks up the backing. If you must lift anything, it should only happen after a test says it's clear.
- **Don't scrape, sand, grind or wire-brush** what's left on the floor. Adhesive and backing residue are the worst things to disturb with a power tool.
- **Don't use a leaf blower or a dry broom or a household vacuum** on a floor that might be affected. Ordinary vacuum cleaners are not built to trap asbestos fibres.
- **Don't put it in a general skip or your household bin.** Asbestos waste has to go to a landfill authorised to receive it, and the only authorised site in the Barwon region is Drysdale Landfill, and you need to call ahead on (03) 5251 2935 before taking anything there. See our [Barwon region disposal guide](/blog/asbestos-disposal-barwon-region/) for more.
- **Don't assume the underside is fine because the top looks fine.**

If you've already started and you've torn up some flooring, stop, keep people and pets out of the room, don't sweep or vacuum, and read our guide on [what to do if you've drilled into asbestos](/blog/drilled-into-asbestos-what-to-do/), which covers the same first steps. If you're worried about exposure, talk to your GP.

## Test first

The only reliable answer is a lab test. A sample analysed by a NATA-accredited laboratory will tell you whether the backing, the underlay or the adhesive contains asbestos. Each layer is a different material, so a floor with lino, underlay and adhesive can mean more than one sample. Most houses need three to eight samples overall, because every different material has to be tested separately.

A lab test typically costs $88 to $160 per sample, as Melbourne-metro benchmarks, and some labs charge a flat fee while others discount from the second sample. Prices are indicative and GST treatment varies between providers, so ask each lab whether its price includes GST. Standard turnaround is one to five business days, with a 25% to 50% surcharge for a 24 to 48 hour result.

Sampling flooring takes care, because the act of cutting a sample is itself a disturbance. Ask whoever takes the sample how they'll do it without breaking up the layer. ## If the test comes back positive

A positive result doesn't mean panic. It means a plan.

If the flooring is intact and in good condition, it can sometimes be left where it is and covered or sealed in some way, but that's a decision to make with a licensed removalist, not a guess on a Saturday morning. If it has to come out, whether it's non-friable or friable decides which licence class is needed.

On the legal side, Victoria's unlicensed limit has two caps that both have to hold: 10 square metres of non-friable material in total and no more than one hour of removal work in any seven-day period. Those are workplace rules. A homeowner at their own home isn't bound by them, because a home isn't a workplace, though WorkSafe recommends following the same code, and the moment a tradie, a tenant, a rental property or a paid helper is involved the caps are law. Friable material is never covered. A whole room of old sheet flooring, and especially one with adhesive residue to deal with, is the sort of job that doesn't suit a weekend. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does old lino contain asbestos?

It can. The backing layer of some old sheet floor coverings was commonly reported as asbestos-containing, but you can't tell by looking at it, and not every old floor does. Only a lab test on a sample can confirm.

### Can carpet underlay contain asbestos?

Old carpet underlay is sometimes listed as a place asbestos may be found, particularly in houses built or extended before 1990. It's not a certainty, so leave it undisturbed and get it tested before lifting it.

### Can I scrape the old adhesive off the floor myself?

Don't scrape, sand or grind it until it's been tested. Adhesive and backing residue can be disturbed easily, and breaking them up is how fibres can end up in the air. If it tests positive, talk to a licensed removalist about what comes next.

### Who puts down the new floor after asbestos flooring is removed?

That's separate work, and you sort it out yourself with a flooring contractor or registered builder. The removalist's job is the removal, and we don't offer replacement flooring.
