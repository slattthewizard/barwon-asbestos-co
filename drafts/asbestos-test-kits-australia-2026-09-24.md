# Asbestos Test Kits in Australia: What You Actually Get, and When a Kit Is the Wrong Call

A mail-in asbestos test kit costs somewhere between $88 and $160 once the lab fee is added, and it answers one question about one piece of material. That is either the cheapest, most honest first step you can take before you touch a fibro fence or a cracked garage roof, or it is the wrong tool entirely, depending on what you are actually looking at and how much of it there is.

This page sets out what an asbestos test kit actually contains, what the result does and does not tell you, and the point at which a kit stops being useful and a proper inspection takes over. Every figure below comes from published Australian sources, and none of it is a quote from us.

## What is actually inside an asbestos test kit

Most kits sold in Australia are mail-in kits, not instant strip tests. There is no home dip-test or colour-change strip that reliably identifies asbestos, because identification depends on examining fibres under a microscope using specialised procedures, which only a laboratory can do. [Asbestos Victoria is explicit on this point](https://www.asbestos.vic.gov.au/about-asbestos/finding-and-identifying/find-and-identify-asbestos-tool): "You can't tell if a material contains asbestos just by looking at it. Only scientific testing of a sample can confirm this."

So what you are buying is really a sampling and postage service. A typical kit includes something to cut or break off a small piece of the suspect material, a sealable bag or container, a form to describe where the sample came from, and a prepaid or pre-addressed way to get it to a NATA-accredited laboratory.

One national mail-in kit publishes a price of $88 including GST, which covers the kit and one lab analysis. Testing extra pieces sent in with the same kit costs $66 each on that provider's published rate. Other labs price by the sample rather than by the kit, at $95 to $160 depending on the provider and whether you send one piece or several.

You are not buying an inspection, a written survey of the property, or anyone coming to look at the material in place. That distinction matters more than the price does.

Either way, get it tested before you touch it further.

## What the result actually tells you, and what it doesn't

A laboratory report says whether asbestos was detected in the specific piece you sent, and if so, which type. It does not say anything about the rest of the house.

This is the part that catches people out. A fence, an eaves lining and a bathroom wall sheet are different materials made at different times, and one result covers only the piece submitted. If a kit ships with one sample bag and you have three suspect materials, you need three samples, not one bag stretched across three jobs. That's covered properly in [how asbestos testing works in Victoria](/blog/asbestos-testing-how-it-works-victoria/), so we won't repeat the lab side of it here.

A test kit result also does not tell you whether the material is friable or non-friable, whether it's in a condition that can be safely removed without a licence, or what a removal job would cost. Those are separate judgements a licensed removalist makes on site, not something a lab report settles from a courier bag.

And a negative result on one sample is not a clean bill of health for the house. It's a clean bill of health for that one piece of material, which is why a single kit falls short when several different materials are in play.

## Who should actually take the sample

Here is where the DIY test kit and official guidance pull in different directions, and it's worth being straight about it. Asbestos Victoria's own guidance on collecting a sample says it should be done by "a competent person", and names three examples: a professional occupational hygienist, a licensed asbestos removalist, or staff at a NATA-accredited laboratory. Homeowners buying a kit and cutting the sample themselves are, in practice, doing the job that guidance says a trained person should do.

There's no law that stops a homeowner from taking a sample of a suspect material at their own home the way there might be for a tradesperson at a workplace. The same logic that applies to small DIY removal jobs applies here: it's a judgement call about a material you're already about to disturb one way or another.

If you go ahead, the same basic precautions apply regardless of who does the cutting. Take the smallest possible piece rather than a large chunk. Wet the surface first rather than cutting it dry. Bag it immediately and seal it, and don't sand, drill, grind or vacuum around it with an ordinary household vacuum.

If the material is already damaged, crumbling, or you're not confident you can get a clean small piece without creating dust, stop and call a professional instead of a kit. That's the line between a job a kit can handle and one that needs [a licensed removalist to look at it first](/blog/can-i-remove-asbestos-myself-victoria/).

## Field scenarios: where a kit fits, and where it doesn't

**A 1960s fibro fence line along the back boundary in Norlane** has one cracked panel near the gate, with the rest of the fence intact. That's a single, easily reached, non-friable sample, and a mail-in kit is a reasonable fit: one sample at $88 to $160 tells you what you're dealing with before anyone touches the rest of the fence. If it comes back positive, whole-fence removal on published Victorian rates runs $1,000 to $3,500, or $20 to $50 per sheet for a few panels, alongside the minimum job charge of $800 to $1,500 that applies no matter how small the job looks.

**Vinyl floor tiles found under carpet in an Ocean Grove holiday house** come up intact, with a dark, brittle adhesive underneath. That's two materials, not one: the tile and the glue, and a kit that includes two analyses, or two samples sent together, can cover this properly. One tile-only sample tells you nothing about the adhesive, and that's the trap a single-sample kit sets if you don't realise you need two.

**A Super Six garage roof in Corio that has started to crack** is where a kit stops being the right tool. The roof is a large area that's already showing damage, and deteriorating corrugated sheet can shed fibres more readily than a sound sheet, so cutting a sample yourself off a roof that's already failing is a worse idea than the $88 to $160 it might save. A licensed removalist or an on-site inspection with sampling, at $395 to $900 on Melbourne-metro rates, is the safer way to find out what's on that roof before anyone gets a ladder near it. ## Why the age of the house still matters, even with a kit in hand

A test kit doesn't need you to know the age of your house to work, but it helps you decide whether to bother. Asbestos Victoria's own likelihood guide puts houses built before the mid-1980s as highly likely to contain asbestos products, houses from the mid-1980s to 1990 as likely, and anything built after 1990 as unlikely. Geelong's post-war Housing Commission suburbs, built for Ford and International Harvester workers, sit squarely in the highly likely band, which is one reason testing before touching anything is the sensible default here rather than the exception. [Where asbestos hides in an older Geelong house](/blog/where-asbestos-hides-old-geelong-house/) covers the room-by-room detail, including where vinyl floor tiles usually turn up.

None of that turns a guess into a result. Age narrows the odds. Only a lab report on an actual sample answers the question, and that's true whether the sample came from a kit or from someone standing on your roof.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does an asbestos test kit actually include?

Most Australian kits are mail-in kits: a tool or bag for taking a small sample, packaging, a form describing where it came from, and a way to send it to a NATA-accredited laboratory. There is no reliable instant strip test. The result comes back from the lab, usually within one to five business days, not on the spot.

### Can I take my own sample for a mail-in test kit?

There's no law against a homeowner sampling a suspect material at their own home, though Asbestos Victoria's guidance is that a competent person, such as a licensed removalist or trained hygienist, should collect it. If you go ahead, wet the material first, take the smallest possible piece, and seal it immediately. Stop and call a professional if the material is already damaged or crumbling.

### How accurate is a DIY asbestos test kit?

The accuracy sits with the laboratory, not the kit itself, because the same microscopic analysis applies whether a professional or a homeowner cut the sample. What a kit can't fix is a poor sample: too small a piece, the wrong spot, or a piece that doesn't represent the material you're worried about. The result only ever answers for the piece that arrives at the lab.

### When should I book a full inspection instead of buying a kit?

Book an inspection when more than one material is involved, the area is large, the material is already damaged or crumbling, or you're dealing with a pre-purchase check or a full renovation. A kit suits one small, intact, easily reached suspect item. Anything bigger or messier is better handled by someone trained to collect the sample and assess the site at the same time.
