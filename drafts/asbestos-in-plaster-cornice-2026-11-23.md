# Asbestos in Plaster and Cornice: Is Your Wall Plasterboard or Fibro?

You're about to knock a hole in a wall, replace a cornice or patch a crack, and a thought crosses your mind: is this plaster, or is it asbestos? It's a good question to ask before the hammer comes out.

Ordinary gypsum plaster and plasterboard aren't generally where asbestos is found. But old houses are a mix, and what looks like plaster can be something else. This is how to think it through, and why a visual check alone isn't enough.

## Is plasterboard asbestos?

Plain gypsum plasterboard, the paper-faced sheet most people picture, isn't generally an asbestos product. The same goes for traditional gypsum plaster applied wet over a base. If your walls are a standard plasterboard job from a house built or renovated after the late 1980s, asbestos in the wall sheet itself isn't what you'd expect.

The catch is that Australian houses built from the mid-1940s until the late 1980s used asbestos cement widely, and many have been extended, patched and repaired over the years with whatever was on hand. So the real question isn't "is plasterboard asbestos?" It's "is this actually plasterboard?" Asbestos was banned in all forms from 31 December 2003, and a house built or extended before 1990 may contain it somewhere.

## What can look like plaster but isn't

Four things regularly catch people out.

**Fibro sheets.** Asbestos cement sheeting was used on interior walls, particularly in wet areas like bathrooms and laundries, in sunrooms and in additions. Once it's painted and the joints are covered, it can look very much like plasterboard. Our guide to [whether your fibro is asbestos](/blog/is-my-fibro-asbestos-hardiflex-villaboard/) covers the sheets by name.

**Textured and sprayed coatings.** Some textured wall and ceiling finishes were commonly made with materials that may contain asbestos. They sit on top of the base layer, so even if the wall is plasterboard, the coating on it might not be. For ceilings in particular, see [textured and popcorn ceilings](/blog/asbestos-ceilings-textured-popcorn/).

**Cornice and cove.** Cornices were sometimes plaster, sometimes plasterboard-faced, and in some older homes fibre cement. Asbestos cornice isn't something you can pick by sight, and the adhesive used to fix cornice can be a separate question again.

**Patching and jointing compounds.** Old filler, cement-based patching and joint compounds may contain asbestos even when the sheet beneath doesn't. A wall that's been repaired several times can have several different materials in one small area.

None of these is certain. Each one is a reason to test before you cut or sand, not a verdict.

## How to tell plaster from fibro (without fooling yourself)

People ask for a quick visual test. There isn't a reliable one. A well-kept asbestos cement sheet can look almost identical to a modern sheet, and a painted wall hides most clues. What you can do is gather hints, then confirm with a lab.

- **The sound.** Tap the wall with a knuckle. Plasterboard tends to sound hollow and light. Fibro sheet is dense and can sound harder and flatter. This is a hint, not a test, and it varies with fixing and framing.
- **Where it is.** Wet areas, sunrooms, enclosed verandahs, laundries and old additions are more likely to have fibro lining than a lounge room in the main part of the house.
- **The joins.** Plasterboard walls usually have taped, set joints that are filled and sanded flat. Fibro often has cover strips, visible joins or small gaps along the sheet edges.
- **The edges.** Where a sheet is exposed, say behind a vanity or around a power point cutout, plasterboard shows a paper face and a soft white core. Fibro shows a dense grey, pale or fibrous core. Don't scrape or break a sheet to look.
- **The age and history.** A house built or extended in the mid-1940s to late 1980s window has higher odds. A lot of Geelong's northern suburbs were built in that window, in estates for workers at Ford, International Harvester and a phosphate works. That's context, not a test.

If the hints point both ways, treat it as unknown. Don't drill, sand, cut or break the wall to find out.

## Why you can't just look at it

Asbestos fibres are far too small to see, and they're mixed into the cement or compound rather than sitting on the surface. Paint, wallpaper, texture coatings and decades of grime hide anything useful. Even experienced tradies get it wrong. The only way to be sure is a lab test on a small sample, analysed by a NATA-accredited laboratory.

For sanding, scraping or cutting an old wall, the risk is dust. If you've already disturbed something and you're not sure what it was, read [what to do in the first hour after drilling into asbestos](/blog/drilled-into-asbestos-what-to-do/). Stop, keep people out, don't sweep, don't vacuum.

## Testing a wall or cornice

Testing is the cheapest step, and it's what tells you whether plaster really is plaster. A lab test typically costs $88 to $160 per sample. Most houses need three to eight samples, because every different material has to be tested separately. A cornice, a wall sheet, a patched section and a textured coating are four samples, not one, even if they turn out to be the same thing.

## What if it does test positive?

Most asbestos cement wall lining is non-friable and falls to a Class B licensed removalist. Damaged, crumbling or sprayed material may be friable, and friable material is always licensed work whatever the amount. Asbestos Victoria says removal is best done by licensed removalists who are trained to do it safely.

A homeowner at their own home isn't bound by the 10 square metre and one-hour workplace caps, because a home isn't a workplace, though WorkSafe recommends following the same code. A tradesperson or paid helper is bound, so any builder you hire should stop work if the wall tests positive. A minimum job in Melbourne-metro terms is $800 to $1,500, which is why a small patch of wall lining costs more than people expect. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is plasterboard asbestos?

Ordinary gypsum plasterboard isn't generally an asbestos product. The risk in older homes is that what looks like plasterboard may be fibro sheet, or may be covered or patched with textured coatings or compounds that may contain asbestos. Only a lab test can confirm.

### Can asbestos be in cornice?

It can be. Cornice in older houses was made from different materials, including plaster and, in some cases, fibre cement, and the adhesive and patching around it are separate questions. You can't tell by eye, so test a sample before cutting or removing it.

### How can I tell if my wall is fibro or plasterboard?

Hints include the sound when tapped, where the wall is, the joins and the exposed edge. None of them is reliable on its own, and paint hides most clues. A lab test on a small sample is the only way to know.

### Is it safe to sand or patch an old plaster wall?

Not until you know what's in it. Sanding creates dust, and a wall in a house built or extended before 1990 may include fibro, textured coating or old compound. Get the materials tested first, and if you have health concerns, talk to your GP.
