# Asbestos in Concrete, Brick and Mortar: What's Actually Worth Worrying About

People ask this a lot, usually just before a slab gets jackhammered or a wall gets knocked through. Is there asbestos in concrete? What about brick, or the mortar between the bricks? The straight answer has two halves. Plain concrete slabs and ordinary bricks are generally not where asbestos turns up. But the stuff built into, under and around them often is, and that's where homeowners get caught out.

Nobody can look at a slab and tell you what's in it, or what's under it. So this article sets out where the real risks sit, what's genuinely uncertain, and why the sensible finish to every one of these questions is a lab test.

## Plain concrete and brick: the short answer

Asbestos cement is a different product to concrete. Asbestos cement sheeting, pipes and similar items were made with asbestos fibres mixed through a cement base, and they were commonly used in residential building from the mid-1940s until the late 1980s. Ordinary concrete, as poured for house slabs and paths, and ordinary fired clay brick, are not normally described as asbestos products.

That's the general picture, not a guarantee. Mortar and some old render, patching compounds and bagged products from the era may have contained asbestos in some cases, and there's no way to tell by eye. If a brick wall or a rendered finish is going to be broken up, drilled heavily or demolished, and the house was built before 1990, it's reasonable to treat "probably fine" as an open question until a sample says otherwise.

So for bricks and mortar on their own, the risk is low but not zero, and it's far smaller than the risk from the materials attached to them.

## Where asbestos actually turns up around concrete and brick

When a house with a concrete slab turns out to have asbestos near it, it's almost always one of these.

**Asbestos cement formwork or sheeting left in or under slabs.** Flat cement sheet is commonly reported to have been used as formwork, to edge a pour or to line a void, and sometimes left in place rather than pulled out afterwards. A slab edge that looks like plain concrete can have a sheet buried in it.

**Fibro packing.** Offcuts of fibro were handy for levelling, shimming and packing things up. You can find it between footings and bearers, under door frames, behind brick piers and in similar tight spots where a thin spacer was needed.

**Old fill under slabs.** Before the ban, building waste was sometimes used as fill, and broken sheet could end up under or beside a slab. Our guide to [asbestos in soil and contaminated fill](/blog/asbestos-in-soil-contaminated-fill/) explains why this is hard to spot and why a surface-only look isn't enough.

**Expansion joint fillers.** The strip of compressible material in a joint between slabs, or between a slab and a wall, is another spot where products from that period may contain asbestos. It's thin and easy to overlook.

**Slabs left behind after a fibro shed or garage comes down.** The sheeting is removed and the owner assumes the job's done. But the slab may carry residue, broken pieces, embedded fixings or debris on and around it. If that slab is going to be cut, broken up or lifted, it deserves its own attention.

None of these makes the concrete itself asbestos. They're things that sit in or under it, and they're the reason demolition crews and excavators get nervous around old slabs.

## What to look for around a slab

You can't test a slab by staring at it, but you can notice the clues that raise the question:

- Flat, grey-white sheets visible at the edge of the slab, in a trench or beside a footing.
- Broken fragments of fibro-like sheet in the garden, along a fence line or beside the slab.
- Fibre-looking, grey or dark strips in expansion joints.
- A slab that was the floor of an old fibro shed, garage or outbuilding.
- Cracked, flaky or powdery patches of old render or patching compound.

Our guide to [what asbestos looks like](/blog/what-does-asbestos-look-like/) goes through the usual visual clues and the many ways they can fool you. If you see broken pieces, don't pick them up, don't move them and don't break them further. Photograph them from a distance, keep people and pets away, and get them tested.

## Why testing is the straight answer here

There's no clean rule of thumb that covers every slab, brick or bag of old mortar. That's the real state of play, and any article claiming a firm yes or no is guessing. What is true is that asbestos is identified by a sample analysed by a NATA-accredited laboratory, and nothing else settles it.

A lab test typically costs $88 to $160 per sample in Melbourne-metro benchmarks, and each different material needs its own sample. Most houses need three to eight samples, because a slab edge sheet, a patch of mortar and a bit of render are separate samples, not one. A standard house inspection with sampling generally runs $395 to $900. Prices are indicative and GST treatment varies between providers, so ask each provider whether their quote includes GST. ## What the law says about renovating and demolishing

Check for asbestos before renovation and demolition work, not after. Our guide to [asbestos before renovating or demolishing in Victoria](/blog/asbestos-before-renovating-demolishing-victoria/) walks through the reasoning. Builders and demolishers working on older properties will generally want to know what's there before they start, and that's a sensible position for a homeowner too.

If someone is doing the work as their job, the Victorian unlicensed removal limits are tight. Non-friable removal without a licence requires both that the area doesn't exceed 10 square metres and that no more than one hour of removal work is done in any seven-day period. Both caps apply together. Those caps apply to work, not to a homeowner at their own home, where WorkSafe recommends following the same code. A whole slab's worth of debris, or a full shed's worth of sheeting, is almost always well past them either way. Friable material is never covered, at any quantity.

## What if you've already broken something up

If you've already smashed a slab, knocked down a wall or dug beside a footing and you now suspect asbestos was involved, stop work. Keep other people away, don't sweep or hose the area and don't put the rubble in a general skip. Asbestos can never go in a general waste skip or a council hard-waste collection. Get a sample tested, and talk to your GP if you're worried about exposure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is there asbestos in concrete?

Plain concrete slabs are generally not where asbestos turns up. The risk is usually in what's attached to or buried near the concrete, such as asbestos cement formwork or sheeting left in or under a slab, fibro packing, expansion joint fillers, old fill and debris from a demolished fibro shed. Only a lab test on a sample can confirm what's in a particular slab or the material around it.

### Is there asbestos in brick or mortar?

Ordinary bricks are generally not described as asbestos products. Mortar, render and some patching compounds from older houses may have contained asbestos in some cases, but you can't tell by looking. If a pre-1990 wall is going to be broken up or heavily drilled, a sample analysed by a NATA-accredited laboratory is the way to find out.

### How much does it cost to remove a concrete slab with asbestos on it?

Slab removal is priced separately by a licensed removalist, and Melbourne-metro benchmarks run roughly $60 to $180 per square metre. There are no published Geelong-specific prices, and the slab may not need to be removed at all if it can be cleared of debris and left in place. Ask each removalist whether their quote includes GST.

### Can I break up an old slab myself if I think it's clean?

If you don't know what's in or under it, test before you break it. Old slabs can have asbestos cement sheeting, fibro packing or fill beneath them, and breaking them up spreads whatever is there. Testing a few samples costs far less than stopping a job midway and dealing with contaminated rubble.
