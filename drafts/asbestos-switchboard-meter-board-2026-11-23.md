# Asbestos Switchboard: What to Do Before Anyone Drills Your Meter Board

The old board that holds your meter and fuses is easy to ignore. It's been on the same wall for decades, it works, and nobody wants to think about it until an electrician says the house needs an upgrade. That's usually the moment the question comes up: is this an asbestos switchboard? In pre-1990 houses, the answer can be yes, and it matters before a single screw goes into it.

This article covers why those boards were often made from asbestos-containing material, what you should never do to one, why your electrician needs to know before they start, and how the work splits between a licensed removalist and a licensed electrician. It also covers the one price that matters for a job this small.

## Why old meter boards can contain asbestos

Asbestos cement products were commonly used in Australian homes from the mid-1940s until the late 1980s. The Premier of Victoria's office has said homes built in Australia until 1990 routinely used asbestos products, and that asbestos is estimated to still be present in one in three homes. That's a national figure, and nobody has published a Geelong one.

Meter boards and switchboard panels were a natural fit for the material. Asbestos-containing boards resist heat and don't conduct electricity, so a flat panel behind the fuses, circuit breakers and meter was a sensible use of it. Many were commonly made from a dense, hard, grey or dark panel, sometimes a bit like a thick slate. Some were backed with a different insulating board again.

Does that mean yours is one? Not necessarily. A board from the 1950s can be asbestos, and a board from a later renovation might be plywood, a plastic panel or a modern product with none. You can't tell by looking. Only a lab test can confirm it.

In Geelong's northern suburbs the odds are a bit higher simply because of when the houses went up. Norlane's Housing Commission program peaked between 1951 and 1957, and Corio gained five estates and about 2,500 houses from the late 1950s. Both sit squarely inside the window when asbestos cement was standard. That doesn't tell you anything about a specific board. It explains why an old meter board in a Corio or Norlane house is worth a second look, and why it's worth taking seriously in any house built or extended before 1990.

## What you should never do to a suspect board

Intact, undisturbed asbestos cement is a much lower risk than damaged material. The real risk comes when it's broken, drilled, sanded, cut or scraped, because that's what releases fibres into the air.

So, with a board you suspect:

- Don't drill it, even for a small cable or a new screw.
- Don't cut it, snap it or lever it off the wall.
- Don't sand, scrape or wire-brush it, and don't blast it with a pressure cleaner.
- Don't let a mate "just have a quick go" at it.

There's a second reason to leave it alone: the board is part of a live electrical installation. Whatever happens to the board, the electrical side is not a DIY job. Keep your hands off it until a licensed electrician has looked at the installation.

If you've already drilled or cut into it, stop and read our guide on [what to do if you've drilled into asbestos](/blog/drilled-into-asbestos-what-to-do/). Don't sweep or vacuum the dust with a household cleaner. Get the area tested and talk to your GP if you're worried about exposure.

## Why your electrician needs to know before work starts

A switchboard upgrade is a common job. It might be triggered by a solar install, an EV charger, a renovation, an insurance request or a safety check. The electrician will usually want to drill, cut, re-fix and re-run cables across and around the board.

That is exactly the work that can disturb an asbestos panel. So the electrician needs to know about your suspicion before they arrive, not when the drill is already running. Tell them if:

- the house was built or extended before 1990
- the board looks like a dense, flat grey or dark panel rather than a timber or plastic one
- a previous owner or inspector has flagged it as possible asbestos

A good electrician will stop, and that's the right call. They'll want a result before they put a tool near it. If you've already had a sample tested and the report says no asbestos detected, you can hand that to them and the job carries on. If the result is positive, the board needs a different plan. Either way you've saved a wasted visit and, more importantly, nobody has breathed in dust.

You can also book the test yourself before you call an electrician at all. Call (03) 5550 4817, or [book a test before you touch it](/#contact). Samples go to a NATA-accredited laboratory for analysis, and you get a written result.

## Who does what: removal versus electrical work

This is where homeowners get tangled, so here's the split.

**The asbestos side.** If the board tests positive, taking it off the wall is asbestos removal work, and it goes to a licensed removalist. Almost all residential work like this is non-friable (bonded) material, which is Class B work. If the board is crumbling or badly damaged, our guide on [friable versus non-friable asbestos](/blog/friable-vs-non-friable-asbestos/) explains why that changes the picture, because friable material needs a Class A licensed removalist at any quantity.

**The electrical side.** Disconnecting, replacing and reconnecting a switchboard is electrical work. That belongs to a licensed electrician, and you engage them directly. This site never offers electrical work of any kind. We'll put you in touch with a licensed removalist for the asbestos side only.

In practice the sequence tends to go like this:

1. Test a sample from the board.
2. If it's positive, get a quote from a licensed removalist, and speak to your electrician about how the two jobs line up. That conversation is between you and each tradesperson.
3. Your licensed electrician deals with isolating and disconnecting the installation as part of their own work, and installs the new board.

Each tradie works under their own licence and your contract is with each of them separately. Our page on [asbestos removal in Geelong](/asbestos-removal-geelong/) explains how the removal side works.

## The price that matters: the minimum job charge

A switchboard is a small item. A board might be well under one square metre, which is why a per-square-metre rate isn't the useful number here. The one that counts is the minimum job charge.

Removal businesses set a minimum because containment, decontamination, paperwork and disposal cost roughly the same whether the job is one board or several sheets. Melbourne-metro benchmarks put that minimum at $800 to $1,500. There's no published Geelong-specific pricing, so treat it as a starting point for a conversation with a licensed removalist rather than a quote.

Prices are indicative and GST treatment varies between providers, so ask each removalist whether their quote includes GST.

Testing is much cheaper. A lab test typically runs $88 to $160 per sample. A switchboard is usually one sample, though if there's also a backing board or old lining around it, those are separate materials and each is tested separately.

One practical tip: if you're also dealing with something else, say a damaged eaves lining or a shed with old sheeting, tell the removalist at the start. Packing small jobs together can make better use of the minimum charge, and it's far better than paying it twice. Our [asbestos removal cost guide](/asbestos-removal-cost/) has the wider price picture.

## Notification and clearance on a job this size

Because a board is well under 10 square metres of non-friable material, the licensed removalist only needs to notify WorkSafe at least 24 hours before starting. A clearance certificate isn't required where the job is non-friable material not exceeding 10 square metres in total. If you're on a larger job that involves more material, that changes, and at your own home any clearance duty sits with the licensed removalist, not with you.

Disposal goes to a landfill authorised to receive asbestos, which the removalist handles as part of their work. You don't need to take it to the tip yourself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can an old meter board really be made of asbestos?

Yes, it can. Asbestos-containing panels were commonly used behind electrical equipment in houses built before the late 1980s because the material resists heat and doesn't conduct electricity. You can't tell by sight, though, so only a lab test on a small sample can confirm whether your board contains asbestos.

### Can my electrician work on the switchboard if it might be asbestos?

They shouldn't drill, cut or fix anything to the board until you know what it's made of. Tell your electrician before they arrive that the board may contain asbestos. Once a NATA-accredited laboratory has analysed a sample, you'll have a written result to hand over, and the right next step depends on it.

### Does the asbestos removalist replace the switchboard?

No. A licensed removalist deals with the asbestos material only. Replacing and reconnecting the switchboard is electrical work, which you engage a licensed electrician to do directly. This site doesn't offer electrical or replacement work.

### How much does it cost to remove an asbestos switchboard?

A board is a small item, so the minimum job charge is the relevant figure. Melbourne-metro benchmarks put that at $800 to $1,500, with no published Geelong pricing available. Prices are indicative and GST treatment varies between providers, so ask each removalist whether their quote includes GST.
