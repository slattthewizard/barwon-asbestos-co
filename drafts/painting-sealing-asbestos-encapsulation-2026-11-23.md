# Painting Asbestos: Can You Paint or Seal Fibro, and What Not to Do Before You Start

Plenty of people look at a weathered fibro wall, a chalky old shed or a faded Super Six roof and think the same thing: just paint it. It's a fair thought. Sealing sound, intact asbestos cement sheeting is a common way to manage it in place, and it's often cheaper than removal. But the paint isn't where the danger is. The danger is everything you do to the surface before the first coat goes on.

This article covers when painting or sealing makes sense, why prep is the risky part, when it's the wrong call, and what you still owe the next person who works on the house.

## Can you paint asbestos cement?

Short answer: yes, in the right condition, and with the right prep. Asbestos cement is a bonded material. The fibres are bound into the cement, and an intact, sound, undisturbed sheet is a far lower risk than a damaged one. The risk comes when the material is broken, abraded, cut, drilled or worn down.

Coating the surface with a suitable paint or sealer is commonly recommended for intact material. The idea behind it, usually called encapsulation, is simple. A film over the surface helps hold the surface in place and cuts down on weathering, so the sheet is less likely to shed dust over time. You're not removing the asbestos. You're managing it where it sits.

That's the honest limit of the idea. Painting doesn't make asbestos safe, and it doesn't make it not asbestos. The sheet underneath is the same sheet. It just has a coating on it.

First, find out if it's asbestos at all. Plenty of fibro-looking sheets are modern fibre cement with no asbestos in them, and plenty of old ones look just like them. Our guide to [what asbestos looks like](/blog/what-does-asbestos-look-like/) covers the clues, but looks only get you so far. A lab test on a small sample is the only way to confirm it. If you'd rather know before you buy a single tin of paint, call (03) 5550 4817, or [book a test](/#contact).

## The real danger is the prep

Paint doesn't grip well to a chalky, mossy, weathered surface, and that's exactly what old fibro tends to be. So the instinct is to clean it back hard first. That instinct is the problem.

Never sand, wire brush, scrape or pressure-wash asbestos cement before painting. Each of those methods breaks up the surface and throws the loosened material into the air or spreads it across the ground, the fence line and the neighbour's yard. Sanding and brushing make fine dust you can't see. Scraping chips off the weathered face of the sheet. Pressure-washing sends contaminated water and debris everywhere, and it can leave a residue in soil and gutters that's hard to deal with afterwards.

Power tools make it worse, and so does the thought that a dust mask from the hardware shop is enough. It isn't. If you've already done any of this, or you've drilled or cut into a sheet, stop and read our guide on [what to do if you've drilled into asbestos](/blog/drilled-into-asbestos-what-to-do/) before you carry on.

So how do people prepare asbestos cement for coating at all? That depends on the condition of the sheet, the coating system and who is doing the work, and it's a question worth putting to a licensed professional rather than guessing. What matters for this article is the rule of thumb: if the plan involves abrading or blasting the surface, the plan is wrong. A gentle approach that keeps the surface wet and undisturbed is the only kind that belongs near asbestos cement, and the product maker's instructions and a professional's advice should be your guide, not a YouTube video.

## When sealing is a reasonable choice

Encapsulation tends to make sense when:

- The sheeting is intact and sound, with no crumbling edges, deep cracks or broken corners.
- The material is non-friable, meaning bonded asbestos cement and not something loose or powdery.
- The sheets are firmly fixed and stable, not sagging, flaking or lifting.
- You're not planning to cut, drill, demolish or renovate that surface any time soon.
- The sheeting is where it can be left alone, such as a fence panel, a shed wall or a roof that isn't being replaced.

If that describes your situation, sealing can be a sensible, affordable middle path between doing nothing and full removal. It's also often a better option than a hurried DIY removal. Our guide to [whether you can remove asbestos yourself in Victoria](/blog/can-i-remove-asbestos-myself-victoria/) explains why the rules around removal exist, and why a whole fence, garage or roof is well past the limits that apply to people doing this as work.

## When encapsulation is the wrong answer

Painting is a poor fit when the material is damaged or friable. Friable asbestos is material that can be crumbled or reduced to powder by hand pressure, like old pipe lagging or some insulation, and it behaves very differently to intact cement sheeting. Our guide to [friable versus non-friable asbestos](/blog/friable-vs-non-friable-asbestos/) explains the difference.

Skip the paint and get advice if you see any of these:

- Sheets that are crumbling, flaking or breaking apart.
- Fire damage, which can turn bonded material into something much more fragile.
- Storm or hail damage with broken or shattered sheeting.
- Edges that are eroded down to loose fibrous material.
- Material that you can pull apart or powder with your fingers.

Damaged or friable material needs a licensed removalist. Friable asbestos is Class A work and always requires a licensed removalist, with no exemption at any quantity. Putting paint over crumbling sheeting hides a problem without solving it, and it can make the next person's job harder because the damage is out of sight.

If you're not sure which category your sheeting falls into, get it looked at before you spend money on coating it. Call (03) 5550 4817, or [get a quote from a licensed removalist](/#contact) and ask them to give an honest view on whether the sheeting can be left in place.

## What it costs to leave it and what it costs to remove it

A coating job is usually cheaper up front than removal, but the comparison isn't as simple as it looks. Painting asbestos doesn't remove the underlying issue, and you may still be looking at removal later, when the sheeting finally gets replaced or the house gets renovated. If your plan is to knock down or alter the wall in the next few years, painting first just adds a cost.

## Painting doesn't cancel your duty to tell the next tradie

This is the part people forget. Once the sheet is painted, it looks like any other painted wall. The next electrician, plumber, carpenter or renovator who drills a hole for a power point or cuts a section out for a window has no idea what's behind the paint.

That's how asbestos gets disturbed years after someone thought they'd dealt with it. If you've had sheeting tested, record the result. Keep the lab report with the house paperwork. Tell any tradesperson before they start, and tell the next owner if you sell. A short written note kept somewhere obvious, such as the switchboard or the paperwork folder, does a lot of good.

It's also worth thinking about the renovation you might do later. Our guide to [asbestos before renovating or demolishing in Victoria](/blog/asbestos-before-renovating-demolishing-victoria/) covers why testing comes before any demolition or major work, and why a coating doesn't change that.

## A quick checklist before you reach for the paint tin

1. Confirm it is asbestos cement with a lab test. Don't assume either way.
2. Check the condition honestly. Intact and sound is one thing, cracked or crumbling is another.
3. Rule out the prep methods that make dust: no sanding, no wire brushing, no scraping, no pressure-washing.
4. Work out whether you're going to renovate, cut or replace that surface soon. If so, painting may be wasted money.
5. Get advice from a licensed removalist if there's any doubt about the condition.
6. Tell every tradie who works on the house about the sheeting, and keep a record.

A sealed wall that's left alone and well documented can be a perfectly reasonable way to live with old fibro. A freshly sanded one is a very different story.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you paint asbestos?

Yes, sound and intact asbestos cement sheeting can be painted, and coating it is commonly recommended for intact material as a way of managing it in place. The risk is in the preparation. Never sand, wire brush, scrape or pressure-wash asbestos cement before painting, because each of those can release fibres or spread contaminated debris.

### Is sealing asbestos the same as removing it?

No. Sealing, also called encapsulation, leaves the asbestos where it is and puts a coating over the surface. The material is still asbestos, and it still needs to be handled properly if anyone later cuts, drills or demolishes it. Removal by a licensed removalist takes the material away for disposal at an authorised landfill.

### Is encapsulation suitable for damaged or crumbling asbestos?

No. Encapsulation is meant for intact, sound, non-friable material. Damaged, crumbling or friable asbestos needs assessment and usually removal by a licensed removalist, because a coating won't hold it together and can hide the problem from the next person who works on the house.

### Do I still need to tell tradespeople if I've painted over asbestos?

Yes. Paint makes asbestos cement look like any other wall, so a tradesperson drilling or cutting into it wouldn't know. Keep your lab test result with the house paperwork, tell anyone doing work before they start, and pass the information on if you sell. Only a lab test can confirm whether a sheet contains asbestos in the first place.
