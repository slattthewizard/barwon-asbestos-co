# Asbestos Fire Damage and Storm Damage: What to Do First, and What Not to Touch

After a bad storm, a hailstorm or a fire, the first thing most people want to do is clean up. Pick up the broken bits, drag the fallen fence panels to the side, hose down the mess. With asbestos cement, that instinct is exactly what you need to fight. Broken, burnt and crumbling sheeting behaves differently to a sheet that's sitting intact on a wall, and the way you handle it in the first hours can make the job a lot bigger.

This guide covers what storm, hail and fire damage can do to old asbestos cement, what to do and not do straight afterwards, and how to get from "there's a mess in the yard" to a proper plan with a licensed removalist.

## What storms, hail and fire do to asbestos cement

Asbestos cement sheeting was commonly used in Australian homes from the mid-1940s until the late 1980s. Roofing, wall cladding, eaves, fences, garage and shed sheeting: if it's old and it's fibre cement, it may be asbestos. You can't tell by looking, and only a lab test can confirm it.

Left alone and in good condition, the fibres are locked up in the cement. That's why it's called non-friable, or bonded. A storm or fire changes the picture in a few ways.

**Wind.** Strong gusts lift roof sheets, snap fixings and push fence panels over. A panel that's landed flat on the lawn might be in one piece, or it might have cracked into a dozen fragments. Either way it's now somewhere it was never meant to be, and people and pets can walk over it.

**Hail.** Hailstones can crack, pit or punch holes through old roof sheeting. Super Six and other corrugated roofing gets brittle with age, and a sheet that's weathered for decades doesn't take an impact well. If you're searching for "asbestos hail damage roof" because you've found broken pieces in the gutters or on the ground below, treat every fragment as suspect until a test says otherwise.

**Fire.** Heat is the worst case. Fire-damaged asbestos cement can crack, spall and turn chalky or crumbly. Sheeting that has been burnt or is crumbling can become friable, meaning it can be crushed by hand and release fibres into the air. Friable material is Class A work in Victoria, and it always needs a licensed removalist, with no exemption at any quantity. Our guide to [friable versus non-friable asbestos](/blog/friable-vs-non-friable-asbestos/) explains the difference.

## Is it actually asbestos?

Plenty of damaged sheeting turns out to be something else. Modern fibre cement contains no asbestos, and it breaks in much the same way. A house built or extended before 1990 may contain asbestos, but that's a rule of thumb, not a legal test, and nobody can tell from a photo.

Until you know, assume it is. That's not drama, it's just the cheapest way to avoid a mistake. A lab test on a small sample is how you find out. If the property is a mess after a fire, don't go collecting samples yourself from burnt or crumbling material. Ask the licensed professional who's sampling to do it, or hold off until the area has been made safe.

## What to do straight after the damage

Here's the order that makes sense in the first day or two.

1. **Stay back and keep others back.** Keep people, kids and pets off the damaged area. If sheeting has come down in a shared space, like a side lane or a common driveway, tape it off or put something heavy at the edges so nobody wanders through.
2. **Don't pick up the pieces.** No bare hands, no shovel, no bucket. Moving broken sheeting is how small fragments become dust. Leave it where it fell.
3. **Don't use a blower or a high-pressure hose.** A leaf blower throws dust and fragments into the air and across the yard. A pressure washer on a cracked roof or a pile of debris does the same thing with water, and it drives fibres into soil, gutters and drains. Both make a contained problem into a spread-out one.
4. **Don't sweep, drive over or mow over it.** Vehicles and mowers crush fragments and throw them. If there are pieces on the lawn, leave them and keep the mower in the shed.
5. **Cover loose material only if you can do it safely.** If rain is coming and you can lay a sheet of heavy plastic over a pile without disturbing it, that's reasonable. If you'd have to step on or drag anything to do it, don't.
6. **Take photos from a safe distance.** You'll want them for your insurer and for any removalist quote.
7. **Wash up and change clothes if you've been in the debris.** If you've already walked through it or handled pieces, don't shake out your clothes inside. Talk to your GP if you're worried about exposure, and then move on to the practical steps.

If you've already got a pile of broken sheeting in the wheelbarrow or a hole in your roof where a section used to be, don't panic. It's a common situation. Stop, leave it, and work through the steps below.

## Talk to your insurer early

If the damage came from a storm or a fire, contact your insurer before you start any clean-up. Ask what they need from you, whether they have a preferred process for asbestos-affected claims, and what documentation they want. We can't tell you what your policy covers, because that depends entirely on the policy and the insurer, so read your own documents and ask them directly.

Keep your photos, any written correspondence and every quote. If your insurer asks for a test result, the lab report is the document to hand over, and the removalist's written quote shows the scope of the job.

## Get it tested and get a licensed removalist

Once the immediate danger has passed, the plan is simple.

**Test first.** A sample analysed by a NATA-accredited laboratory is the only way to confirm what the sheeting is. Samples should be taken carefully, ideally by someone who does this for a living, and there's no need to rush a sample before the area has been made safe. **Then get a quote from a licensed removalist.** Broken and fire-damaged sheeting is rarely a weekend job. The Victorian limit for unlicensed removal is 10 square metres of non-friable material and no more than one hour of removal work in any seven-day period. Both caps have to hold. They're workplace rules, and a homeowner at their own home isn't covered by them, but WorkSafe still recommends homeowners follow the same code, and the caps become law the moment a tradesperson, a tenant, a rental property or a paid helper is involved. A damaged roof, a downed fence line or a burnt garage is usually past 10 square metres, and fire-damaged material may be friable, which has no unlicensed exemption at all.

If your property was damaged by fire and the sheeting is crumbling, the removal is Class A work. Class A licence holders can do both Class A and Class B work. Class B licence holders are limited to non-friable material only, so the licence class matters when you're comparing quotes. ## How storm-damaged roofing is usually handled

If the damage is to an asbestos cement roof, the conversation is a bit different to a fallen fence. A damaged Super Six roof may have loose sheets, cracked ridge capping and broken pieces in the gutters and downpipes. Our guide to [Super Six asbestos roof removal](/blog/super-six-asbestos-roof-removal/) goes through what's involved, and our [asbestos roof removal page](/asbestos-roof-removal-geelong/) covers the Geelong side of things.

For fences, [asbestos fence removal in Geelong](/asbestos-fence-removal-geelong/) is the place to start. A fence that's come down in the wind is often easier to deal with than one that's still standing and cracking, but only if nobody has dragged it around the yard or run a mower over the corners.

Whatever the damage, a few things hold true:

- The removalist must notify WorkSafe before starting. That's 24 hours for 10 square metres or less of non-friable material, and five days for anything else. It's one reason a job can't usually start tomorrow, even after a storm.
- Disposal has to go to a landfill authorised to receive asbestos, with packaging and labelling that meets the rules. The removalist handles that as part of the job.
- Air monitoring is tied to Class A (friable) work. It isn't a routine part of a Class B job.
- Re-roofing, re-cladding and rebuilding the fence are separate jobs. You arrange them with a registered builder, not with us and not with the removalist.

## What not to do, in one place

- Don't handle, bag or carry damaged sheeting yourself without proper advice.
- Don't blow, sweep, hose or pressure wash the area.
- Don't drive, walk or mow over fragments.
- Don't put it in your household bin, a general skip or a council hard-waste collection. Asbestos has to go to a landfill authorised to receive it.
- Don't let anyone start demolition or clean-up work, including a mate with a trailer, until you know what the material is.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is broken asbestos sheeting after a storm dangerous?

It can be. Intact asbestos cement is generally a lower risk than material that's been broken, crushed or burnt, because breaking it can release fibres. Treat every fragment as suspect until a lab test says otherwise, keep people and pets away, and don't pick it up or clean it with a blower or pressure washer. If you're worried about exposure, talk to your GP.

### Can I clean up fire-damaged asbestos sheeting myself?

You shouldn't. Fire-damaged or crumbling sheeting can become friable, and friable asbestos is Class A work that always needs a licensed removalist, with no exemption at any quantity. Leave it alone, keep people away, and get a test and a quote from a licensed removalist.

### Should I tell my insurer about asbestos before I clean up?

Yes, contact your insurer before you start any clean-up, and take photos from a safe distance first. What's covered depends on your policy, so ask them directly what they need from you and keep copies of every quote and message.

### How long before a licensed removalist can start after storm damage?

It depends on the removalist's availability, but there's a fixed step first. The licence holder has to notify WorkSafe at least 24 hours before starting for 10 square metres or less of non-friable material, or at least five days before for any other removal work. Ask each removalist for a realistic start date when you get quotes.
