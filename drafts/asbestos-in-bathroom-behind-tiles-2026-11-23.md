# Asbestos in Bathroom Walls and Floors: What's Behind the Tiles

Most people looking at old bathroom tiles are thinking about the tiles. Ugly colour, cracked grout, a shower that never quite drains. The material behind the tiles gets a lot less attention, and in a house built before 1990 that's where asbestos in the bathroom is most likely to be. Nothing shows from the front. You find out when the hammer goes in.

This article covers what's behind the tiles in wet areas, why a renovation is the risky moment, when to test, how much sheet a typical bathroom adds up to, and how the job splits between a licensed removalist and the builder who rebuilds the room.

## Where asbestos hides in wet areas

Asbestos cement was commonly used in Australian housing from the mid-1940s until the late 1980s, and wet areas were one of its main uses. It coped with damp better than the plasterboard of the day, so it tended to go where water went:

- **Bathroom walls.** Flat sheets fixed to the studs, with tiles stuck straight onto them.
- **Bathroom floors.** Sheet flooring under tiles or under a later layer of vinyl, sometimes under a screed.
- **Laundries.** Wall sheeting around the tub and the washing machine, plus the floor.
- **Kitchens.** Splashback areas behind the stove and sink, where a tiled or sheet-lined wall may have a fibre-cement base.
- **Ceilings and eaves nearby.** Often the same era, and sometimes the same product.

It isn't in every room of every old house, and a house that's been renovated may have a mix of old and newer sheeting. You can't tell by looking at the finished tile surface, and you can't tell the sheet by sight even once exposed, because asbestos cement looks very like modern fibre cement. Only a lab test can confirm it.

In Geelong's northern suburbs, the odds are higher simply because of timing. The Norlane Housing Commission program peaked between 1951 and 1957 and reached 2,464 houses by 1976, and Corio gained five estates and about 2,500 houses from the late 1950s. That's all inside the window when asbestos cement was standard. Nobody has published how many of those houses contain it, so the point is only that it's worth checking.

## Why a bathroom renovation is the risky moment

Intact asbestos cement behind tiles isn't doing anything. The danger starts when somebody breaks it.

Stripping tiles is rough work. Tiles are stuck down with adhesive, and the quickest way off is a hammer and bolster, a scraper or an angle grinder. When the tile comes away it often takes the surface of the sheet with it, or cracks the sheet underneath. That puts dust and fragments into the air in a small, enclosed room, and it spreads through the house on shoes, clothes and tools.

Removing the old sheet is the next step in most renovations, and that means cutting, snapping and levering. A single bathroom demolition can create a lot of broken material before anyone realises what it is.

So the plan has to be: test first, demolish second. If you've already started and you've found sheeting you don't recognise, stop work, keep people out of the room and see our page on [what to do if you've drilled or broken asbestos](/blog/drilled-into-asbestos-what-to-do/). If you're worried about exposure, talk to your GP.

## Test before demolition

The cheapest step in a bathroom job is also the one most often skipped. A sample from the wall sheet, another from the floor and one from any wall lining beside it goes to a NATA-accredited laboratory for analysis, and you get a written result.

A lab test typically costs $88 to $160 per sample. Most houses need three to eight samples because every different material is tested separately. A bathroom wall sheet, a floor sheet and an eaves lining are three samples, not one. A full house inspection with sampling generally runs $395 to $900, and standard turnaround is one to five business days.

Taking a sample disturbs the material, so it's best done by someone who samples for a living rather than with a screwdriver on a Saturday. Call (03) 5550 4817, or [book a test before you touch it](/#contact). Our guide on [how asbestos testing works in Victoria](/blog/asbestos-testing-how-it-works-victoria/) covers the process in detail.

Tell your builder and plumber about the test result before they start. If it's clear, the job goes ahead. If it's positive, you need a different plan.

## How much sheet is in a typical bathroom?

This is the number that surprises people. Say you have a small bathroom with four walls and a floor. Add up the wall areas and the floor area, and it can come to well past 10 square metres of sheeting before you count the laundry or the kitchen splashback. Even if only some walls are sheeted, you can pass it quickly.

That matters because of how Victoria's rules work. Work classed as "limited asbestos removal" has two caps that both have to hold: no more than 10 square metres in total, and no more than one hour of removal work in a seven-day period. Those are workplace rules that bind employers and self-employed people doing removal work as a job. They don't bind a homeowner at their own home, because the compliance code says a home isn't regarded as a workplace, but WorkSafe recommends homeowners follow the same code. The caps become law if a tradesperson, a tenant, a rental property or a paid helper is involved. A builder or tiler on your bathroom is exactly that case.

Our guide on [whether you can remove asbestos yourself in Victoria](/blog/can-i-remove-asbestos-myself-victoria/) has the detail. For a bathroom, the short version is that it's probably more sheet, and more breakage risk, than a weekend job.

## How a licensed removalist handles wet-area sheeting

Almost all residential bathroom sheeting is non-friable (bonded) asbestos cement, which is Class B work. Where it's damaged or crumbling, or where sanding and grinding have already turned it to dust, friable rules can apply and a Class A licensed removalist is needed. Our guide on [friable versus non-friable asbestos](/blog/friable-vs-non-friable-asbestos/) explains the difference.

What a licensed removalist does, in general terms:

- Seals the room off from the rest of the house and uses wet methods to keep dust down.
- Takes the sheet off carefully, in pieces rather than snapped, ideally with tiles still attached to avoid breaking them.
- Wraps and labels the waste, and takes it to a landfill authorised to receive asbestos. The only one on EPA Victoria's register in the Barwon region is Drysdale Landfill.
- Cleans the area and notifies WorkSafe before starting: at least 24 hours for 10 square metres or less of non-friable material, and at least five days for anything else.

Where more than 10 square metres of non-friable material is removed, a clearance certificate from an independent person is needed before the area is reoccupied. At your own home, the duty to get it sits with the licensed removalist you engaged, not with you.

## The new bathroom is separate work

Once the old sheeting is out, you're looking at a bare frame. The new bathroom, with new sheeting, waterproofing, tiling and fittings, is domestic building work. This site doesn't offer it or quote for it. You hire a registered builder for that, and a licensed plumber and electrician for their parts, each under their own contract with you.

It's worth talking to the builder before removal so they know the room will be bare and when it'll be ready. Getting the sequence right between the removalist and the builder is between you and each of them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is there asbestos behind my bathroom tiles?

It's possible in a house built or extended before 1990, because asbestos cement was commonly used for wet-area wall and floor sheeting. You can't tell from the tile surface, and the sheet itself looks much like modern fibre cement. A lab test on a small sample is the only way to know.

### Is it safe to remove the tiles before testing?

It's best not to. Stripping tiles often cracks or breaks the sheet beneath, which can release fibres into a small room. Test a sample before any demolition so you and your builder know what you're dealing with.

### Can asbestos be in a kitchen or laundry too?

Yes. Laundries often have the same wet-area sheeting as bathrooms, and kitchen splashback areas may sit on fibre-cement sheet. Each different material is tested separately, which is why most houses need three to eight samples.

### Does the removalist rebuild the bathroom?

No. A licensed removalist deals with the asbestos only. The new bathroom is separate building work, which you engage a registered builder to do directly, along with a licensed plumber and electrician where needed.
