# Asbestos Water Pipes, Gutters and Downpipes Around Older Houses

When people think about asbestos at home, they think about walls and roofs. But asbestos cement was also pressed, moulded and rolled into pipes, gutters and downpipes, and a lot of it went into the ground and onto the edge of the house where nobody looks. It tends to be found by accident: a plumber digs up a drain, an excavator catches a pipe, or a gutter cracks and the broken edge looks suspiciously like fibro.

This article covers where asbestos cement pipes and rainwater goods were used, what to do the moment one turns up, and why the plumbing replacement afterwards is a separate job with a different tradesperson.

## Why asbestos cement was used for pipes and gutters

Asbestos cement is asbestos fibre bound into cement. It was commonly used in the manufacture of residential and commercial building products from the mid-1940s until the late 1980s, and the total ban on all forms of asbestos came into force on 31 December 2003.

It doesn't rot, so it suited things that sat in wet ground or out in the weather. Alongside the sheeting, you'll find it in:

- **Stormwater pipes**, carrying roof and surface water away from the house.
- **Sewer and drainage lines**, including the run from the house to the street connection.
- **Gutters and downpipes** on the house itself.
- **Flues and vent pipes**, usually near a heater or hot water service.

Geelong's northern suburbs were built in bulk inside exactly that window. The Housing Commission estates at Norlane and Corio went up from the 1950s, much of the housing for workers at Ford, International Harvester and a phosphate works. That doesn't tell you what's in any individual yard. It does explain why a pipe dug up in an older suburb deserves a second look.

## How to recognise it

You can't be sure by sight, and it's worth saying that plainly. Asbestos cement pipe is typically grey, a bit chalky, heavy and rigid, with a smooth rolled finish and a thick wall compared to plastic. A broken end can show a pale, fibrous-looking edge. Old gutters and downpipes in asbestos cement tend to be thicker, more brittle and more prone to cracking than the metal or plastic types, and they're often painted over many times.

None of that is proof. Modern pipes can look similar, and plenty of old pipes are clay, concrete or steel. Our guide to [what asbestos looks like](/blog/what-does-asbestos-look-like/) covers the usual clues and their limits. The only way to know for certain is a sample analysed by a NATA-accredited laboratory.

## What to do when a plumber or excavator finds a pipe

This is the situation that matters most, because the damage is usually done in the first few minutes.

1. **Stop work.** Switch off the machine or tool. Don't keep digging to see what it is.
2. **Don't cut, snap or break it.** In particular, don't cut it with a power saw, grinder or angle cutter. Power cutting asbestos cement makes a large cloud of fine dust.
3. **Keep people away.** Children, pets and anyone not wearing suitable protection should be moved out of the area.
4. **Don't sweep it up or hose it.** Dry sweeping lifts dust, and hosing spreads contaminated water and debris.
5. **Leave it covered and undisturbed.** If it's broken, cover it with plastic sheeting and put a few weights on the corners. Don't bag fragments yourself.
6. **Get it tested.** Call (03) 5550 4817, or [book a test](/#contact).

## Buried pipes versus pipes above ground

Where the pipe sits changes the risk and the response.

**Intact pipe buried in the ground and left alone** is generally considered low risk, because nothing is disturbing it. Many people choose to leave a sound underground pipe where it is. The problem is that "left alone" ends the moment someone needs to dig, repair or connect to it.

**Pipe that's cracked, broken or crumbling** is a different matter. Fibres can be released, particularly if the material is dry and brittle, and any disturbance makes it worse.

**Gutters and downpipes** are above ground and exposed to weather, so they weather and crack. Old asbestos cement gutters can break up when you clean them, when a ladder is leant on them or when a downpipe is knocked. Don't scrub, scrape or pressure-wash them to clean them up. Weathering that has roughened a surface is a good reason not to scrub it.

## Who does the removal, and why it isn't the plumber

Asbestos cement is a bonded, non-friable material when intact, and in Victoria most residential work of this kind is Class B work. Class B covers non-friable asbestos. Friable material, which includes anything crumbling to powder, is Class A and always needs a licensed removalist, with no exemption at any quantity. Our guide to [friable versus non-friable asbestos](/blog/friable-vs-non-friable-asbestos/) explains the difference.

This is where people get mixed up. A plumber is licensed for plumbing. That's a different thing to being a licensed asbestos removalist. Removing asbestos cement pipe, as work, needs the right licence unless it falls inside the limited unlicensed work caps: no more than 10 square metres of non-friable material in total, and no more than one hour of removal work in any seven-day period. Both caps have to hold together, and friable material is never covered. Those caps bind people doing the work as a job, not a homeowner at their own home, where WorkSafe recommends following the same code. A run of buried pipe is easy to take past both limits.

The practical sequence is:

1. Stop and test.
2. If it's asbestos, [get a quote from a licensed removalist](/#contact) for the removal and disposal.
3. The removalist notifies WorkSafe before starting. That's at least 24 hours before for 10 square metres or less of non-friable material, and at least five days for anything else.
4. Once the removal is done, the plumber or drainer comes in to put new pipe in.

That last step is separate work. Replacing the stormwater line, sewer connection, gutter or downpipe is plumbing, and you'd engage a licensed plumber directly. We don't offer or quote it.

## Disposal

Asbestos waste has to go to a landfill authorised to receive it, wrapped and labelled properly. In the Barwon region, the only facility on EPA Victoria's register is Drysdale Landfill, phone (03) 5251 2935, small domestic quantities only. It's not accepted at any Surf Coast Shire transfer station. Ring ahead before you go anywhere. A licensed removalist handles disposal as part of the job, and our [disposal guide for the Barwon region](/blog/asbestos-disposal-barwon-region/) explains the rules in more detail.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Were asbestos cement pipes used for stormwater and sewer lines?

Yes. Asbestos cement was commonly used in building products from the mid-1940s until the late 1980s, and that included pipes for stormwater, drainage and sewer lines as well as gutters and downpipes. You can't identify one by sight alone, so a sample analysed by a NATA-accredited laboratory is the way to confirm it.

### What should I do if a plumber finds an asbestos pipe?

Stop work, switch off any machine or power tool, keep people away and don't cut or break the pipe. Don't sweep or hose the area. Cover any broken pieces with plastic, get a sample tested and, if it's asbestos, get a quote from a licensed removalist. Don't cut it with a power saw.

### Are asbestos gutters and downpipes dangerous if I leave them alone?

Intact, sound material that isn't disturbed is generally considered lower risk, but old gutters and downpipes weather, crack and break easily. Don't scrub, scrape or pressure-wash them. If they're cracked or crumbling, have them tested and talk to a licensed removalist about taking them down.

### Who replaces the pipe after the asbestos is removed?

That's a separate job. A licensed removalist deals with the asbestos, and you then engage a licensed plumber directly to install new pipes, gutters or downpipes. We don't offer or quote plumbing replacement.
